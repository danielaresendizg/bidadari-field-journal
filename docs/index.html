Orientation

Bidadari Park

Bidadari Park sits in the Toa Payoh/Serangoon area of Singapore, at the heart of the Bidadari public housing estate. This site is the paired field patch for a Field Journal assignment for LA5222 (Urban Ecology and Design, NUS), completed jointly by Daniela Resendiz Garcia and Janelle, with a field visit carried out on 5 September 2026.

This page assembles, in chronological and thematic order, what can be verified from academic and official sources about the site, organised so that the historical, planning, and social/ecological strands required by the assignment can be read alongside the pair's own field observations. Where a detail could not be confirmed against a citable source, the underlying research notes flag it explicitly as unconfirmed rather than stating it as fact; this page presents only the material that was verified.

The sections that follow move from the site's layered history (royal estate, multi-religious cemetery, housing estate, park), through the water policy and engineering that shape Alkaff Lake, to the 271 georeferenced field photographs collected on site and a short synthesis connecting the data to course themes.

Method

Our Mosaic

The water patch

Within Bidadari Park, this Field Journal narrows its focus to one specific mosaic, the water system centred on Alkaff Lake, following the patch-corridor-matrix structure of landscape ecology (Forman & Godron, 1981, "Patches and Structural Components for a Landscape Ecology," BioScience, as covered in Week 2). Alkaff Lake itself is the patch, a bounded body of water distinct from the vegetation around it. The Ficus Stream and the drainage lines feeding it are the corridors, a special type of patch that links other patches in the matrix and can serve as habitat, a movement route for species, a barrier or filter, or a source of environmental and biotic effects. The surrounding park vegetation forms the matrix. Together, this patch plus its connecting corridor within the matrix is our mosaic, a representative sample of the wider landscape rather than the whole park.

This focus was chosen deliberately around the theme of water, following Jasenka Čakarić's 2010 paper "Water Phenomenon, Urban Morphology Transformation" (Facta Universitatis), which treats water as a principal generative element of urban form. Čakarić's model proposes an "optimum sustainable percentage" of water surface relative to total urban area, ranging from 3.66% to 25.81%. Alkaff Lake (~1.8 ha) relative to the Bidadari Park core (~10 ha) gives a water coverage of roughly 18%, which falls within that range, a concrete point of contact between the site and the reference theory.

History

Condensed from the field-journal historical timeline (academic, NHB, NLB and HDB/URA/NParks sources)

1870s–1885
Istana Bidadari
Before it was a cemetery, the land held the Istana Bidadari, home to Sultan Abu Bakar of Johor's consort Cecilia Catharina Lange (Che Puan Besar Zubaidah); her children, including future Sultan Ibrahim, were born there in 1871 and 1873.
1902–1910
A Muslim cemetery is established
Land first sought for a Christian cemetery was granted only on condition that adjoining land be reserved for a Muslim burial ground. The Muslim section opened on 14 February 1910, making it Singapore's first Muslim state cemetery.
1929–1964
Alkaff Gardens
A separate, roughly 10-acre private leisure garden built by the Alkaff family opened to the public in 1929, with boating, pavilions and bamboo groves. Requisitioned by the military in 1941 and bombed in WWII, it was later sold, used as a film location, and finally filled in 1964. It is the direct heritage referent for today's Alkaff Lake.
1907/1908–1972
Bidadari General Cemetery in operation
One of Singapore's largest cemeteries, with Christian, Muslim, Hindu and Sinhalese sections, operating until closure on 31 October 1972. By closure it held an estimated 147,000 graves (roughly 54,000–58,000 Christian and 68,000–78,800 Muslim).
1996
Redevelopment earmarked
Government announces the former cemetery land is set aside for future housing, opening a long period of public debate.
2001–2006
Exhumation
Graves exhumed in stages under religious supervision; process considered complete by 2006. Most graves went unclaimed (by end-2001, ~49,000 of ~58,000 Christian graves alone). Remains were cremated, then re-interred at Choa Chu Kang or scattered at sea.
2004–2019
First Bidadari Memorial Garden
NHB establishes a memorial garden reusing the original cemetery gates and 21 relocated headstones of notable individuals. Closed in June 2019 to make way for construction; artefacts relocated into the new park.
2013
Bidadari Estate announced
HDB announces Bidadari as a new housing estate (~10,000–12,000 units). Urban Strategies Inc., MKPL Architects and Arup engaged as master planners under a "garden-like" vision integrating housing, park and green corridors.
2014
URA Master Plan designation
Formalises a ~10-hectare Bidadari Park, a Heritage Walk along the former Upper Aljunied Road's rain trees, Singapore's first underground air-conditioned bus interchange, and an ecolink to Alkaff Lake/Hillock Park.
2013–2024
Construction under "Community in a Garden"
HDB delivers housing and public realm in phases with NParks, PUB and NHB. In 2024 the estate master plan won a FIABCI World Gold award, Singapore's only one that year.
3 September 2024
Bidadari Park and Alkaff Lake open
NParks, HDB, PUB and NHB jointly open the park and its centrepiece, Alkaff Lake, a stormwater retention pond (up to 40,000 m³, ~16 Olympic pools) that doubles as recreational space, with a Heritage Walk and the relocated Memorial Garden.
2024
Ecological design features
A "respect, transform, beautify" design combines heritage with biodiversity infrastructure, including a marshland with variable water depths, the cascading Ficus Stream constructed wetlands, a land bridge to an adjacent hillock as a bird/butterfly habitat corridor, and upcycled timber for insect and amphibian habitat.

Policy

From national catchment policy to the treatment train at Alkaff Lake

Singapore scale

The Four National Taps strategy draws on local catchment, imported water, NEWater and desalinated water, a diversification approach dating to the 1972 Water Master Plan.

Water catchment covers roughly two-thirds of Singapore's land area, draining through canals and drains to 17 reservoirs; PUB aims to expand this toward 90% of land area by 2060. Marina Barrage (2008) and Marina Reservoir (2010) anchor the island's largest, most urbanised catchment and pushed national catchment from one-half to two-thirds of land area.

The ABC Waters Programme, launched in 2006, integrates drains, canals and reservoirs into the built environment via constructed wetlands, vegetated and bioretention swales, rain gardens and sedimentation basins arranged as a "treatment train" that progressively slows, detains and cleanses runoff.

Alkaff Lake, site scale

Alkaff Lake is an ABC Waters retention pond of 1.8 ha, holding ~10,000 m³ in dry weather and up to 40,000 m³ (~16 Olympic pools) during storms, with water level rising from 1–1.5 m to ~4 m. Runoff from ~43.5 ha (about half the estate) is diverted here, with sensors and beacons warning the public during flood events.

Terraced wetlands filter this water using plants such as Umbrella Papyrus, Pickerelweed, Narrowleaf Cattail and Alligator Flag, which capture sediments and nutrients while lake water is periodically recirculated through the wetlands and aerated to raise dissolved oxygen.

A Gross Pollutant Trap intercepts litter and coarse debris upstream of the wetlands. This is the first stage of the same treatment-train logic used at national scale, applied here in one small, documented catchment feeding into Marina Reservoir.

The biotic set (59 photos) is dominated by non-native ornamental species such as Antigonon leptopus, Lantana camara and Duranta erecta, reflecting the strong footprint of tropical landscape horticulture on a designed park's "wild-looking" planting. A smaller native core also appears, including Cheilocostus speciosus, Blechnopsis orientalis, and Lepironia articulata (a Singapore species once thought nationally extinct, rediscovered in 2014). The abiotic set (168 photos) spans a wide mix of subtypes, including pathways and paved surfaces, rock/hardscape, bare soil, structures and bridges, the water body itself, constructed wetland, vegetation-management traces, and signage, underscoring how much of the patch's physical fabric is engineered rather than "natural."

The most striking single finding is Ardisia elliptica, native to Singapore (and much of tropical Asia) yet documented as an aggressively invasive species in Florida and the Pacific. The same plant is "native" or "invasive" purely depending on which side of a biogeographic boundary it is growing on, which is a concrete illustration that invasiveness is a geographically relative status, not a fixed property of a species, and a useful counterweight to any instinct to read "native" as simply "good" and "introduced" as simply "bad."

Read together with the human-experience photos (21 photos, including jogging, dog-walking and photographing) and the water infrastructure covered under Policy, the patch reads as a site where engineered and spontaneous ecology sit side by side. A retention pond and terraced wetlands built explicitly for stormwater function also host native wetland flora and fauna; ornamental plantings imported for aesthetics coexist with pockets of genuine native biodiversity; and everyday recreational use overlays all of it. This is close to the tension the course frames around designed urban nature, where a landscape that is legible as "green" and ecologically functional remains a thoroughly engineered artefact of planning, drainage policy and horticultural choice rather than an untouched remnant.
